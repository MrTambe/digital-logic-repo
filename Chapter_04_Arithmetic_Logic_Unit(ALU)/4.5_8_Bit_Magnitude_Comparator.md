# ⚖️ 4.5 8-Bit Magnitude Comparator

While adders and subtractors perform arithmetic, a CPU also needs the ability to make logical decisions (e.g., "If $A$ is greater than $B$, jump to a new line of code"). This requires a dedicated circuit called a **Magnitude Comparator**.

A Magnitude Comparator takes two binary numbers ($A$ and $B$) as inputs and determines their relative size. It outputs its decision on three mutually exclusive wire lines: **Greater Than ($A > B$)**, **Equal To ($A = B$)**, and **Less Than ($A < B$)**.

---

## 🧠 4.5.2 Multi-Bit Comparison Logic

When comparing multi-bit numbers (like `1011` vs `1001`), the hardware doesn't need to look at every bit simultaneously to make a decision. Just like humans reading decimal numbers, the hardware evaluates starting from the **Most Significant Bit (MSB)** down to the **Least Significant Bit (LSB)**.

    <td align="left" valign="top" style="padding: 10px; font-size: 0.95em;">
      <b>The Rules of Multi-Bit Comparison:</b><br>
      Let $X_i$ represent the equality of a specific bit position ($X_i = A_i \text{ XNOR } B_i$).
      <ol>
        <li><b>For A = B:</b> Every single bit must match. <br>
        $Output_{A=B} = X_3 \cdot X_2 \cdot X_1 \cdot X_0$</li>
        <li><b>For A > B:</b> Either the MSB is greater, OR the MSB is equal <i>and</i> the next bit is greater, and so on cascading downward.<br>
        $Output_{A>B} = (A_3 \cdot \overline{B_3}) + (X_3 \cdot A_2 \cdot \overline{B_2}) + (X_3 \cdot X_2 \cdot A_1 \cdot \overline{B_1}) \dots$</li>
      </ol>
      <br>
      Because these equations get massively complicated and require heavy gate arrays (similar to Carry-Lookahead logic), engineers package 4-Bit Comparators into dedicated IC chips (like the classic <b>7485 IC</b>).
    </td>
  </tr>
</table>

## 🔗 4.5.3 Building the 8-Bit Comparator (Cascading)

To compare two 8-bit numbers (e.g., $A[7:0]$ and $B[7:0]$), we do not build an entirely new 8-bit circuit. Instead, we **cascade two 4-bit comparator blocks together**. 

To allow this chaining, a 4-bit comparator chip includes three special input pins: $I_{A<B}$, $I_{A=B}$, and $I_{A>B}$. These are used to listen to the results of a lower-order chip.

      <b>Architecture Setup (Using Two 4-Bit Blocks):</b>
      <ul>
        <li><b>Block 1 (Lower Nibble - LSB):</b> Compares bits $0 \dots 3$. Because it is the lowest order block, its cascade input pins are hardwired to a default state: $(I_{A=B} = 1)$, $(I_{A>B} = 0)$, $(I_{A<B} = 0)$.</li>
        <li><b>Block 2 (Upper Nibble - MSB):</b> Compares bits $4 \dots 7$. Its cascading input pins are directly wired to the output pins of Block 1.</li>
      </ul>
      <br>
      <b>How the Decision is Made:</b><br>
      The <b>Upper Nibble (Block 2)</b> has absolute authority. 
      <ul>
        <li>If $A[7:4] > B[7:4]$, Block 2 outputs <code>Greater</code> and completely ignores Block 1.</li>
        <li>If $A[7:4] < B[7:4]$, Block 2 outputs <code>Lesser</code> and completely ignores Block 1.</li>
        <li><b>The Tie-Breaker:</b> If the upper four bits are perfectly identical ($A[7:4] = B[7:4]$), Block 2 defers to its cascading inputs. It will output whatever Block 1 tells it to output.</li>
      </ul>
