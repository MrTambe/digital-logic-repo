# ➕ 4.3 8-Bit Adders & Carry-Lookahead

An **8-Bit Adder** is an arithmetic circuit designed to add two entire bytes of data simultaneously. It accepts two 8-bit operands, $A [7:0]$ and $B [7:0]$, alongside an initial Carry-In ($C_{IN}$), and outputs an 8-bit Sum $S [7:0]$ and a final Carry-Out ($C_{OUT}$). 

In decimal terms, it computes $(A + B + C_{IN})$, handling maximum values of $255 + 255 + 1 = 511$. Because 511 requires 9 bits to represent (binary `111111111`), the 9th bit is pushed out through the $C_{OUT}$ pin, which the CPU flags as an **Overflow** or uses to chain into a 16-bit operation.

There are two primary ways to build an 8-bit adder: the cheap, slow way (**Ripple-Carry**) and the complex, fast way (**Carry-Lookahead**).

---

## 🌊 4.3.1 The 8-Bit Ripple-Carry Adder (RCA)

The most intuitive way to build an 8-bit adder is simply to string eight Full Adders together in a chain, exactly how you would do addition by hand on a piece of paper—column by column, passing the carry to the left.

      While hardware-efficient, RCAs are incredibly slow. Full Adder 7 (the MSB) <i>cannot compute its final answer</i> until Full Adder 6 finishes and provides its carry. FA 6 is waiting on FA 5, all the way down to FA 0.
      <br><br>
      If one Full Adder takes roughly <b>2 gate delays (ticks)</b> to produce a carry, an 8-bit RCA takes $8 \times 2 = 16 \text{ ticks}$ to stabilize. 
      <br><br>
      Scaled up to a 64-bit modern CPU, this would take 128 gate delays just to do basic addition. At gigahertz clock speeds, the CPU simply cannot wait this long.

## ⚡ 4.3.2 The Need for Speed: Carry-Lookahead Logic (CLA)

To solve the crippling delay of the Ripple-Carry Adder, engineers invented the **Carry-Lookahead Adder (CLA)**. Instead of waiting for carries to ripple through each gate sequentially, a CLA uses advanced Boolean algebra to calculate the carries for *all columns simultaneously* before the addition even begins.

It relies on two new signals generated instantly by the inputs: **Generate ($G$)** and **Propagate ($P$)**.

<table width="100%">
  <tr>
    <th width="35%" align="center">🎬 Generate & Propagate Logic</th>
    <th width="65%" align="center">📊 The Lookahead Mathematics</th>
  </tr>
  <tr>
    <td align="center" valign="top" style="padding: 10px;">
      <img src="media/cla_pg_generator.gif" alt="Generate and Propagate Block" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 8px;" />
      <br><br>
      <i style="font-size: 0.9em;">The CLA strips out the standard Carry chains and replaces them with a Lookahead Logic Unit.</i>
    </td>
    <td align="left" valign="top" style="padding: 10px; font-size: 0.95em;">
      <b>1. Generate ($G$):</b> A column will <i>guarantee</i> a Carry-Out if both $A$ and $B$ are 1, regardless of what the previous column does.<br>
      $G_i = A_i \cdot B_i$<br><br>
      <b>2. Propagate ($P$):</b> A column will <i>pass along</i> an incoming Carry if exactly one of its inputs is 1.<br>
      $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$<br><br>
      <b>Unrolling the Equation:</b><br>
      Because we know $G$ and $P$ immediately, we can predict future carries using massive flattened OR/AND gates. For example, Carry 2 ($C_2$) will be `1` if:<br>
      <ul>
        <li>Column 1 Generates a carry: $G_1$ <b>...OR...</b></li>
        <li>Column 0 Generates, and Column 1 Propagates it: $P_1 \cdot G_0$ <b>...OR...</b></li>
        <li>There was a $C_{IN}$, and both columns Propagate it: $P_1 \cdot P_0 \cdot C_{IN}$</li>
      </ul>
      <b>Boolean Equation for $C_2$:</b><br>
      $C_2 = G_1 + (P_1 \cdot G_0) + (P_1 \cdot P_0 \cdot C_{IN})$<br><br>
      <i>Result: $C_2$ is computed in just 2 gate delays, rather than waiting for Column 0 to finish adding!</i>
    </td>
  </tr>
</table>

## 🏗️ 4.3.3 Building the 8-Bit CLA (The Hybrid Approach)

In a perfect world, we would write a massive Carry-Lookahead equation for all 8 bits at once. However, the equation for $C_8$ would require an 8-input AND gate and a 9-input OR gate, which is too physically massive (High Fan-In) to etch into silicon efficiently.

Therefore, modern hardware typically builds **4-Bit CLA Blocks** (like the famous `74283` Integrated Circuit) and cascades two of them to create an incredibly fast 8-Bit Adder.

      <ul>
        <li><b>Standard RCA Delay:</b> ~16 Gate Ticks</li>

        <li><b>Hardware Cost:</b> Significantly higher gate count, requiring lots of multi-input AND/OR gates inside the Lookahead unit.</li>
      </ul>
      <b>The Takeaway:</b> In digital logic design, you are constantly trading silicon real estate (more logic gates) for speed. 
    </td>
  </tr>
</table>

# ➖ 4.5 Dedicated 1-Bit Subtractors (Half & Full)

While modern CPUs use 2's Complement addition to perform subtraction (as covered in Section 4.4), purpose-built digital circuits sometimes require dedicated subtraction hardware without the overhead of 2's complement conversion. 

A **1-Bit Subtractor** calculates the difference between two binary bits. Just like addition produces a Sum and a Carry, binary subtraction produces a **Difference ($D$)** and a **Borrow ($B_{OUT}$)**. If you try to subtract `1` from `0`, you must "borrow" a `1` from the next most significant column.

---

## 📉 4.5.1 The Half Subtractor

The **Half Subtractor** handles the subtraction of two single-bit inputs: the Minuend ($A$) and the Subtrahend ($B$), calculating $A - B$. It is called a "Half" subtractor because it cannot accept a Borrow-In from a previous column, making it only useful for the very first bit (the Least Significant Bit) of a multi-bit operation.

### 🛠️ Building the Full Subtractor

Just as a Full Adder is created by combining two Half Adders, a **Full Subtractor is constructed by wiring two Half Subtractors together**.

      <b>Wiring Steps (The Cascading Architecture):</b>
      <ol>
        <li><b>Stage 1:</b> Calculate the initial subtraction of $A - B$ using the first Half Subtractor. This produces a <i>partial difference</i> and a <i>partial borrow</i>.</li>
        <li><b>Stage 2:</b> Take that partial difference and subtract the $B_{IN}$ from it using the second Half Subtractor. The output here is the true, final <b>Difference ($D$)</b>.</li>
        <li><b>Borrow Logic:</b> A borrow could have been triggered in Stage 1 <b>OR</b> Stage 2. Wire both partial borrows into an <b>OR Gate</b> to produce the final <b>Borrow-Out ($B_{OUT}$)</b>.</li>
      </ol>
      <b>Chaining:</b> To build an N-Bit Ripple-Borrow Subtractor, you chain these Full Subtractors together exactly like an RCA, connecting the $B_{OUT}$ of Bit 0 to the $B_{IN}$ of Bit 1.
