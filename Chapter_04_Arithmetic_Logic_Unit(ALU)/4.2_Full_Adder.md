# ➕ 4.2 Full Adders

While a Half Adder is perfectly fine for adding the very first column of a binary number (the 1s column), it completely fails for the rest of the math because it cannot accept a carry-over from a previous column. 

Enter the **Full Adder**. This circuit solves the chaining problem by introducing a third input pin: **Carry-In ($C_{IN}$)**. By accepting $A$, $B$, and $C_{IN}$, the Full Adder can correctly calculate the sum of any middle column in a multi-bit addition problem, producing a final **Sum ($S$)** and a **Carry-Out ($C_{OUT}$)** to pass to the next column.

---

Because the Full Adder has three inputs, our truth table must account for $2^3 = 8$ possible combinations. The maximum possible addition is $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ (which is `11` in binary: Sum = `1`, Carry = `1`).

## 🛠️ 4.2.2 Hardware Implementation

You do not need to invent new logic from scratch to build a Full Adder. The name "Full Adder" is a literal description of its construction: **it is made by wiring two Half Adders together**, joined by a single OR gate.

  <tr>
    <td align="center" valign="top" style="padding: 10px;">
      <img src="media/full_adder_gates.gif" alt="Full Adder built from Two Half Adders" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 8px;" />
      <br><br>
      <i style="font-size: 0.9em;">Two Half Adders cascading their results, with an OR gate catching the Carry.</i>
    </td>
    <td align="left" valign="top" style="padding: 10px; font-size: 0.95em;">
      <b>Wiring Steps (The 2-Stage Process):</b>
      <ol>
        <li><b>Stage 1 (Half Adder 1):</b> Feed $A$ and $B$ into the first Half Adder. This calculates a <i>partial sum</i> ($A \oplus B$) and a <i>partial carry</i> ($A \cdot B$).</li>
        <li><b>Stage 2 (Half Adder 2):</b> Take the partial sum from Stage 1 and add it to $C_{IN}$ using the second Half Adder. The sum output of <i>this</i> stage is your final, true <b>Sum ($S$)</b>.</li>
        <li><b>Carry Logic:</b> Either Stage 1 generated a carry, OR Stage 2 generated a carry (they will never both generate a carry simultaneously). Feed both partial carries into an <b>OR Gate</b> to produce the final <b>Carry-Out ($C_{OUT}$)</b>.</li>
      </ol>
      <b>Gate Depth:</b> <code>2 to 3 Ticks</code><br>
      The data must cascade through multiple gates. The final $C_{OUT}$ takes slightly longer to compute than a single Half Adder.<br><br>
      <b>Component Cost:</b> 2 XOR, 2 AND, 1 OR.
    </td>
  </tr>
</table>

### 🔗 4.2.3 The Ripple-Carry Adder (Chaining them together)

Now that we have an arithmetic unit that accepts a Carry-In and produces a Carry-Out, we can finally build a multi-bit calculator (like an 8-bit or 32-bit ALU).

    <td align="left" valign="top" style="padding: 10px; font-size: 0.95em;">
      <b>Building a 4-Bit Adder:</b><br>
      To add two 4-bit numbers together ($A_3A_2A_1A_0 + B_3B_2B_1B_0$), you line up four Full Adders in a row. 
      <br><br>
      You simply wire the $C_{OUT}$ pin of Column 0 directly into the $C_{IN}$ pin of Column 1. Then Column 1's $C_{OUT}$ goes to Column 2's $C_{IN}$, and so on.
      <br><br>
      <b>The Latency Problem:</b><br>
      This architecture is called a <b>Ripple-Carry Adder</b> because the carry signal "ripples" through the circuit. Column 3 <i>cannot</i> calculate its final answer until Column 2 finishes, which is waiting on Column 1, which is waiting on Column 0. In a 64-bit modern CPU, waiting for 64 adders to ripple sequentially would be far too slow, leading to the invention of "Carry-Lookahead" circuits.
    </td>
  </tr>
