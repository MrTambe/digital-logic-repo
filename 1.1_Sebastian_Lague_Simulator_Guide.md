\## The Art of Abstraction



To build our machine, we are using Sebastian Lague's Digital Logic Simulator. It is a masterpiece of minimalist engineering, and it enforces the most important concept in all of computer science: \*\*Abstraction.\*\*



When you first open the simulator, you are given almost nothing. You have input switches, output lamps, and exactly two logic gates: \*\*AND\*\* and \*\*NOT\*\*. That is it. 



Do not be fooled by the barren interface. Every digital device in human history can be constructed from those two components. 



\### The Interface

1\. \*\*Inputs (Toggles):\*\* These are your environmental variables. You flip them to send a '1' (High voltage) or '0' (Low voltage) into the wire.

2\. \*\*Outputs (Lamps):\*\* These allow you to read the state of the circuit. If the logic resolves to True, the lamp glows.

3\. \*\*Wires:\*\* The red lines. They carry the state instantly from an output pin to an input pin. 



\### Creating Custom Chips (Bottom-Up Design)

You cannot build an 8-bit computer on a single screen using raw AND/NOT gates; your brain would melt from the visual noise. You must encapsulate complexity.



When you wire an AND gate and a NOT gate together to create a NAND gate, you will use the simulator's packaging feature to turn that mess of wires into a single, clean block labeled \*\*"NAND"\*\*. 



Once you package a chip, \*\*you never need to look inside it again.\*\* You treat it as a black box. You trust that if you give it specific inputs, it will yield the correct output. 

\* We will package gates to make adders.

\* We will package adders to make an ALU.

\* We will package the ALU into a CPU.



This is bottom-up design. Always verify a chip works flawlessly before packaging it, because a single bad wire in Chapter 2 will cause a catastrophic system failure in Chapter 10.
