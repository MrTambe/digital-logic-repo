\## Data Density



As we progress into more complex combinational logic, standard truth tables become unwieldy. A component with just 4 inputs has 16 possible states. A component with 8 inputs has 256 states. Reading a single, infinitely long vertical column is a poor use of space and human focus.



To solve this, throughout this course, we will use a \*\*Compact 5x2 Matrix Format\*\* for our foundational truth tables.



\### The Structure



Instead of listing all states in a single vertical column, we split the table horizontally. The left side handles the first half of the binary counting sequence, and the right side handles the second half.



Here is the static template we will use for any standard 2-input gate (like AND, OR, XOR):



| Inputs | Output | Inputs | Output |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| \*\*A  B\*\* | \*\*O\*\* | \*\*A  B\*\* | \*\*O\*\* |

| 0  0 | \*State 1\* | 1  0 | \*State 3\* |

| 0  1 | \*State 2\* | 1  1 | \*State 4\* |



\### How to Read It



1\. \*\*A\*\* and \*\*B\*\* represent our input pins.

2\. \*\*O\*\* represents the final output state of the chip.

3\. Read the left column first, from top to bottom (Binary `00` and `01`).

4\. Then read the right column, from top to bottom (Binary `10` and `11`).



This format trains your eyes to recognize binary counting patterns (0, 1, 2, 3) across columns, allowing you to instantly memorize the "shape" of a gate's logic rather than brute-forcing it. As we move to 3-input and 4-input systems, these tables will expand modularly, keeping the data dense, clean, and easily scannable on modern screens.

\## The Foundation of All Compute



In this chapter, we forge the basic elements of the digital universe. Everything in a modern CPU—from the arithmetic logic unit calculating floating-point math to the memory registers storing your operating system—is built entirely from the logic gates contained in the table above.



In Sebastian Lague's simulator, you are given only two of these by default: \*\*AND\*\* and \*\*NOT\*\*.



This is not a limitation; it is an architectural lesson. With just those two gates, we possess the power of \*Universal Logic\*. We can derive every other gate in existence.



\### What You Will Build Here



We will progress systematically through the primitives. For each gate, we will explore:



1\. \*\*The Definition:\*\* What does this gate actually \*mean\* in human logic?

2\. \*\*The Derivation:\*\* How do we construct it using only the gates we have built in previous steps?

3\. \*\*The Implementation:\*\* We will wire it, test it, and package it into a custom chip to be used forevermore.



\### The Power of Inversion



As you step through this chapter, pay close attention to the gates ending in \*\*"N"\*\* (NAND, NOR, XNOR). The "N" stands for \*\*NOT\*\*. It simply means taking the output of a standard gate and running it through an inverter. While this seems like a trivial difference, you will soon discover that NAND and NOR are the secret structural load-bearers of modern computing.



Click through to \*\*2.1 NOT Gate\*\* to begin building your standard library.
